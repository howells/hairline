# Golden-age statistical graphics (1780s–1950s): visual craft and public-domain examples

Research date: 2026-10-13. Labels used below:

- **[doc]**: documented in a cited source.
- **[obs]**: my own direct observation of the saved scan. The file is named so you can check it.
- **[inf]**: inference.

Strictly, Friendly dates the "Golden Age" to c.1850–1900. The 1780s–1850 period is its prelude, and 1900–1950 is what he calls the "modern dark ages". Both are covered here because the brief asked for them.

## Key works and makers

### Takeaway

There is a clear line of descent. Playfair's hand-coloured copperplate charts (1786/1801) came first. Minard's lithographed flow maps (1840s–1869) followed. State albums and atlases then turned the craft into a house style: Cheysson's _Album de Statistique Graphique_ (1879–1897) and the US Statistical Atlases (1874, 1883, 1898). Du Bois's hand-painted 1900 plates sit at the end of that tradition. Brinton (1914), the Joint Committee (1915), Isotype (1925–34 onward) and Fortune (1930s–50s) mark the shift to cheap one-ink printing, written rules and pictograms.

### Cited Findings

**Periodisation**

- Friendly dates the Golden Age to "roughly 1850 and 1900". He calls the first half of the 20th century the "modern dark ages" of data visualisation. — [Friendly 2008, Statistical Science 23(4)](https://www.datavis.ca/papers/golden-STS268.pdf)
- Friendly says graphics lost status as statistics turned to numbers and formal inference. He quotes Kruskal (1978) on "a contagious snobbery against so unpopular, vulgar and elementary a topic as graphics". — [Friendly 2008](https://www.datavis.ca/papers/golden-STS268.pdf)

**Printing technology**

- Copperplate engraving "could easily accommodate fine lines, small lettering, stippled textures". [doc] — [Friendly 2008](https://www.datavis.ca/papers/golden-STS268.pdf)
  - Playfair's charts of 1786 and 1801 "were printed via copperplate and hand-colored, and therefore printed in limited numbers".
  - Lithography (Senefelder, 1798) was cheaper, allowed longer runs and "made it easier to achieve fine tonal gradation in filled areas".
  - By c.1850 it was adapted to colour printing, "permitting color to be used as an important perceptual feature".

**Playfair**

- _The Commercial and Political Atlas_ was published in London in 1786 (Wallis). Its subtitle reads "by Means of Stained Copper-Plate Charts". — [Wikipedia: William Playfair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Playfair)
  - It contains the Scotland bar chart: one year (1780–81) of imports and exports for 17 partners, drawn as 34 bars, because he lacked time series.
  - Wikipedia gives two counts in different places: 43 time-series plots plus one bar chart, and "34 plates". It is internally inconsistent.
- The _Statistical Breviary_ (1801) holds what is probably the first pie chart and circle graph. — [Wikipedia: William Playfair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Playfair)
- The Denmark & Norway plate is imprinted "Published as the Act directs, 1st May 1786, by Wm Playfair" and "Neele sculpt 352 Strand, London". So the engraver was Neele, not Playfair. [obs] — [Commons file](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:1786_Playfair_-_12_Exports_and_imports_to_and_from_Denmark_%26_Norway_from_1700_to_1780.jpg)

**Minard**

- Minard produced 63 known graphic works between 1843 and 1869. His term _carte figurative_ signals that geography was secondary. — [Friendly 2008](https://www.datavis.ca/papers/golden-STS268.pdf)
- In the 1863 cotton map he "widened the English Channel and the Strait of Gibraltar, and reduced the coastline of North America to a mere cartoon form" to make room for the flow bands. — [Friendly 2008](https://www.datavis.ca/papers/golden-STS268.pdf)

**Nightingale**

- Her polar-area "Diagram of the causes of mortality in the army in the East" was published in _Notes on Matters Affecting the Health, Efficiency, and Hospital Administration of the British Army_ (1858). — [Commons: Nightingale-mortality.jpg](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Nightingale-mortality.jpg)
- A companion plate's own title states the colour code: "Zymotic Diseases (Blue), from Wounds &c (red), and from all other Causes". — [Commons/David Rumsey](<https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Diagrams_Representing_the_Relative_Mortality_from_Zymotic_Diseases_(Blue),_from_Wounds_%26c_(red),_and_from_all_other_Causes_(IA_dr_diagrams-representing-the-relative-mortality-from-zymotic-diseases-blue-10564004).jpg>)

**Cheysson's _Album de Statistique Graphique_**

- A bureau of graphic statistics was set up in the Ministry of Public Works in March 1878 under Émile Cheysson (1836–1910). — [Friendly 2008](https://www.datavis.ca/papers/golden-STS268.pdf)
  - The _Album_ ran 1879–1897 as large quartos (about 11 × 15 in), with fold-outs at "four or six times that size, all printed in color and with great attention to layout and composition".
  - Each volume had 12 to 34 plates, most with 21–23.
  - Most plates are cartograms and Minard-style flow maps, often with "double- or multiflow lines".
  - It was discontinued after 1897 "due to the high cost of production".
  - Funkhouser (1937) called the Albums "the finest specimens of French graphic work in the century".

**US Statistical Atlases**

- The 1874 atlas (Ninth Census, director Francis A. Walker) was "the first true U.S. national statistical atlas", with 54 plates. — [Friendly 2008](https://www.datavis.ca/papers/golden-STS268.pdf)
  - It introduced bilateral histograms (age pyramids) and mosaic/treemap plates.
  - The 1883 atlas (Hewes and Gannett) had "nearly 400 thematic maps and statistical diagrams composed in 151 plates". The 1898 atlas (Gannett) had 126 plates.
  - These volumes used "trellis-like collections of shaded maps", which are small multiples.
  - The 1910 and 1920 atlases "were both routinized productions, largely devoid of color and graphic imagination" (Dahmann 2001).

**Du Bois, 1900**

- The Library of Congress catalogues the plates as "Chart prepared by Du Bois for the Negro Exhibit of the American Section at the Paris Exposition Universelle in 1900". — [Commons/LOC metadata](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_Georgia_Negro_LCCN2005676812.jpg)
  - Physical description: "1 drawing : ink and watercolor ; 710 x 560 mm (board)". Some sheets are "ink and photomechanical print" or "ink and wash".
  - Part of the Daniel Murray collection.
- Public Domain Review gives "fifty-eight" hand-drawn charts, "Created by Du Bois and his students at Atlanta". It lists the rights as "PD U.S." / LOC "no known restrictions". — [Public Domain Review](https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/w-e-b-du-bois-hand-drawn-infographics-of-african-american-life-1900/)

**Brinton**

- _Graphic Methods for Presenting Facts_ (Engineering Magazine Co., 1914) was aimed at non-statisticians: "Mathematics have been entirely eliminated." — [Wikipedia: Willard Cope Brinton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willard_Cope_Brinton)
- Brinton chaired the Joint Committee on Standards for Graphic Presentation, formed 1913, which issued a preliminary report in 1915. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willard_Cope_Brinton); [report text, ASA Publications 14 (1915/16) pp.790–797](https://archive.org/details/jstor-2965153)
- _Graphic Presentation_ (Brinton Associates, 1939) has chapters on "Crosshatched and Colored Maps" and "Color and Its Use". Brinton says it "would not have been feasible except for the photo offset process of reproduction and color printing", and that "many of the charts here shown in color were originally black and white". — [search summary of book](https://archive.org/details/graphicpresentat00brinrich) (I did not read the scan myself)

**Isotype**

- Isotype began as the Vienna Method of Pictorial Statistics at the Gesellschafts- und Wirtschaftsmuseum, 1925–34, and was renamed Isotype c.1935. — [Wikipedia: Isotype](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotype_(picture_language)>)
  - Otto Neurath was its theorist. Gerd Arntz made the pictograms (full-time from 1929). Marie Neurath was the "transformer".
  - _Gesellschaft und Wirtschaft_ (1930) was a collection of 100 charts.
  - Its catch-phrase: "To remember simplified pictures is better than to forget accurate figures."

**Mulhall**

- Michael G. Mulhall's _Dictionary of Statistics_ was published by Routledge in 1884. Catalogue and bookseller descriptions list the 1884 edition at 504 pp. with 16 colour illustrations. — [search summary, NLI/Open Library/Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/mulhallsdiction00unkngoog)
- Bátorfy (2026) treats it as an early step in pictorial statistics that "still uses schematic pictograms". — [Bátorfy, "Hickmann's influence"](https://attilabatorfy.substack.com/p/hickmanns-influence)

**Fortune**

- The first issue (Feb 1930) carried a manifesto promising "a clear and readable text profusely illustrated with pictures". — [Eye magazine, "Wheels of Fortune"](https://eyemagazine.com/feature/article/wheels-of-fortune)
  - Colour was at its best in "the pictorial maps and diagrams", printed by gravure on heavy uncoated stock, each "within a 4pt solid black border".
  - From 1938 Brennan wanted a format for "the growing use of charts and diagrams".
  - 1942–45 brought "a renewed emphasis on charts, diagrams and maps", with Herbert Bayer's science diagrams.
  - Will Burtin was art director 1945–49, with flat and 3D flow charts "notable for their use of simple formal elements and colour".

### Inferences

- [inf] Each era's look follows its reproduction process:
  - Copperplate plus hand colour gave Playfair fine engraved lines with flat watercolour washes.
  - Colour lithography gave Minard, Cheysson and the US atlases tint gradations and many inks.
  - One-ink line blocks and Ben Day screens gave Brinton hatching and mechanical tints.
  - Gravure and offset gave Fortune and Brinton (1939) flat spot colour.
- [inf] What a modern designer reads as "vintage feel" is largely those process constraints, not deliberate styling.

## Specific techniques (line weight, hatching, stipple, lettering, legends, frames, small multiples, paper, two-colour, monochrome categories)

### Takeaway

Across 170 years the craft is consistent:

- A strict weight hierarchy: heavy data, hairline grid, emphasised baseline or reference line.
- Labels written onto the marks rather than in keys.
- Categories coded by texture (hatching, stipple, ruled tints) when only one ink was available.
- Repeated identical panels for comparison.
- A ruled frame and a full descriptive title.

The 1915 Joint Committee wrote most of these down as rules.

### Cited Findings

**Line-weight hierarchy**

- The Joint Committee's 1915 rules [doc] — [ASA report, archive.org](https://archive.org/details/jstor-2965153):
  - (5) "The zero lines of the scales for a curve should be sharply distinguished from the other coordinate lines."
  - (6) For percentage curves, "emphasize in some distinctive way the 100 per cent line".
  - (9) "not to show any more coordinate lines than necessary to guide the eye".
  - (10) "The curve lines of a diagram should be sharply distinguished from the ruling."
- Brinton, 1914 [doc] — [Brinton 1914 full text, Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/graphicmethodsfo00brinrich):
  - He criticises a chart where "The 100 per cent line has not been made any heavier than the other lines".
  - He makes the key US population curve "much heavier than any of the others, so that it may be brought prominently to the eye".
  - He praises grids printed in green ink that read "much lighter in color than the black drawing ink used for the scales and the curves".
  - He notes map borders often have to be gone over "by hand ... to make those particular lines very much heavier" so they survive photographic reduction.
- In Playfair's Denmark & Norway plate (1786) [obs]:
  - The two data curves are thick and coloured. The grid is a fine single-weight hairline.
  - The £100,000 horizontal is ruled heavier and labelled "100,000", acting as a reference line.
  - The plate is enclosed in a double-rule frame (thick outer, thin inner).

**Fills: hatching, stipple and mechanical tints as category codes**

- Brinton's 1914 book was printed in one ink: "color printing is prohibitive on account of the cost". The illustrations "show what can be done in printing complex charts with only one color of ink". — [Brinton 1914](https://archive.org/details/graphicmethodsfo00brinrich)
  - On superimposing textures: "It is not easy to make a clear black-and-white drawing if one kind of cross-hatching must be placed on top of another kind ... it is possible, however, to superimpose two kinds of cross-hatching and get a drawing that is fairly clear".
  - He covers the "Ben Day method of mechanical shading".
  - He describes a state-rank map with "Light Shading ... Dark Shading ... and Black" for quartiles, which is an ordered monochrome scale.
- Playfair's Scotland bar chart (1786) separates exports and imports with no colour, using solid black bars against "ribbed" (ruled/hatched) bars. The caption reads "The Black Lines are Exports the Ribbed lines Imports". [obs from plate caption; verify at full zoom] — [Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:1786_Playfair_-_Exports_and_Imports_of_Scotland_to_and_from_different_parts_for_one_Year_from_Christmas_1780_to_Christmas_1781.jpg)
- The 1874 Statistical Atlas, Plate III (Woodland, compiled by Wm. H. Brewer, 1873), uses one green ink in "five degrees of density". [obs]
  - The legend is a vertical stack of five small boxes, I–V, each with its numeric range ("Under 40 acres to the sq. mile", "40–120" … "360 and over").
  - Coastlines carry engraved horizontal water-lining (concentric offset lines), which is cartographic ornament rather than data. — [Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Statistical_atlas_of_the_United_States_based_on_the_results_of_the_ninth_census_1870_with_contributions_from_many_eminent_men_of_science_and_several_departments_of_the_government_LOC_05019329-29.jpg)
- Friendly notes a Galton weather map with "Stippled and hatched backgrounds". In Bertillon's Paris atlas (1889/91), one category is "cross-hatched" alongside pink and yellow. — [Friendly 2008](https://www.datavis.ca/papers/golden-STS268.pdf)
- In Brinton's 1914 Fig. 4 (pp. 6–7), each component of a single stacked bar gets a different one-ink texture (stipple, ruled, solid). Labels are set inside each block, and there are two scales (tons left, dollars right). [obs] — [Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Graphic_methods_for_presenting_facts,_p._6-7.jpg)

**Direct labelling and lettering**

- Joint Committee rules (1915) — [ASA report](https://archive.org/details/jstor-2965153):
  - (16) "All lettering and all figures on a diagram should read from the base as the bottom, or from the right-hand edge".
  - (13) scale figures go "at the left and at the bottom or along the respective axes".
  - (14) "It is often desirable to include in the diagram the numerical data".
  - (17) "The title of a diagram should be made as clear and complete as possible."
- Brinton (1914) objects to pie charts partly because "the direction of the lettering must be reversed as the eye proceeds around the circle". The horizontal-bar method "lends itself more readily to the use of type so that the reader may read type statements without having to turn the book". — [Brinton 1914](https://archive.org/details/graphicmethodsfo00brinrich)
- Playfair (1786) [obs]:
  - Series names are engraved in small italic along the curve ("Line of Imports", "Line of Exports"), following its slope. There is no legend.
  - The areas between the curves carry large spaced italic capitals: "BALANCE AGAINST" and "BALANCE in FAVOUR of ENGLAND".
  - A one-line scale note sits under the frame: "The Bottom line is divided into Years, the Right hand line into £10,000 each".
  - The value scale is on the right.
- Minard, Russian campaign (1869) [obs]: troop numbers are written directly beside the flow band at intervals. The temperature chart below is tied to the map by thin vertical drop-lines, with temperatures and dates lettered at each drop. — [Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Carte_figurative_des_pertes_successives_en_hommes_de_l%27arm%C3%A9e_fran%C3%A7aise_dans_la_Campagne_de_Russie_1812-13.jpg)
- Du Bois plates (1900) [obs]:
  - Titles are hand-lettered in even, spaced, monoline capitals, centred at the top.
  - Values are written at the ends of or inside the bars, e.g. on "Acres of land owned by Negroes in Georgia".
  - Legends are small filled circles beside short capital labels.
  - Large areas of unpainted buff board are left as ground.

**Small multiples**

- Friendly: lithography and photography fed "what Tufte (1983a) later called 'small multiples'". The US census atlases used "trellis-like collections of shaded maps". — [Friendly 2008](https://www.datavis.ca/papers/golden-STS268.pdf)
- Scribner's 1883 atlas, "Presidential elections. Grant-1872, Hayes-1876, Garfield-1880" [obs]: three identical horizontal strips, one per election. Each has the same layout: area chart, state-by-state bar list and small US map in the same palette, so the reader compares down the page. — [Commons](<https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Presidential_elections._Grant-1872,_Hayes-1876,_Garfield-1880._Copyright,_1883,_by_Charles_Scribner%27s_Sons._(IA_dr_presidential-elections-grant-1872-hayes-1876-garfield-1880-copyright-1-4521010).jpg>)
- Scribner's 1883 "Selected classes, by states, 1880" [obs]: a dense grid of tiny identical horizontal-bar panels, one per state, on a shared scale, in essentially one ink. — [Commons](<https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Selected_classes,_by_states,_1880._Based_on_the_returns_of_the_tenth_census._Copyright,_1883,_by_Charles_Scribner%27s_Sons._(IA_dr_selected-classes-by-states-1880-based-on-the-returns-of-the-tenth-census-4521032).jpg>)
- 1898 Statistical Atlas, Plate 3, "Population of the United States ... 1790 to 1820" [obs]: four maps in a 2 × 2 grid (Distribution in 1790, 1800, 1810, 1820). They share projection, extent, a sequential brown density scale and a legend in each panel. The plate title is set in light sans-serif capitals. — [Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Statistical_atlas_of_the_United_States,_based_upon_the_results_of_the_eleventh_census_LOC_07019233-15.jpg)

**Colour use (limited palettes, two-colour)**

- Nightingale used three hues for three causes (blue, red, black, per the companion plate title). — [Commons/David Rumsey](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Nightingale-mortality.jpg)
  - [obs] The large dominant category is pale blue. The darker inks are kept for the smaller categories, so the wedges overlap legibly.
  - [obs] The two roses (April 1854–March 1855, April 1855–March 1856) are joined by a dashed connector. An explanatory paragraph is set beneath, in place of a legend box.
- Friendly describes a Cheysson plate using "a bipolar color scale" (red below mean, yellow above). He also describes Swiss 1897 plates that colour-link a pie slice to a bar chart decomposing it. — [Friendly 2008](https://www.datavis.ca/papers/golden-STS268.pdf)
- Minard, 1869 [obs]: two colours carry the whole story, tan for the advance and black for the retreat. The same plate pairs a Hannibal map (top) with the Napoleon map (bottom) for comparison.
- Minard, 1858 butcher's-meat map [obs]: proportional pie charts are placed on supplying départements. Départements are flat-tinted yellow or grey-brown, and pie sectors use three inks.
- Cheysson, 1883 Paris traffic census [obs]: one blue, with band widths on the street plan proportional to traffic. Red is held back for the boxed tables and notes.
- Cheysson, 1881 omnibus receipts [obs]: a different saturated ink per omnibus line on a pale plan, with a red ring for the central zone.
- Du Bois [obs]: a small recurring palette of crimson, gold/yellow, blue, black, tan/brown and grey on buff board. — [Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_Georgia_Negro_LCCN2005676812.jpg)
- Brinton (1939) ranked 14 text/background colour pairs by visibility, black on yellow best and blue on red worst, and showed colour-blind views. — [search summary citing Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/3052263/this-1939-chart-explains-how-color-affects-legibility) (secondary source)

**Borders, frames, titles**

- [obs] Playfair, the US atlases and Cheysson all use ruled frames, typically a heavy outer rule with a thin inner rule. The atlases add a graticule tick band between the rules, and plate numbers sit outside the frame ("Pl. III", "Plate 20").
- Fortune: each colour illustration sat "within a 4pt solid black border". — [Eye](https://eyemagazine.com/feature/article/wheels-of-fortune)

**Paper tone**

- [obs] Every scan shows warm cream-to-buff stock. Du Bois's boards are a mid buff, and the unpainted board acts as a colour in the design.
- [inf] Much of the warmth is age-toning and scanner white balance, not the original paper. Treat "paper tone" as an inspiration for a warm off-white ground, not a colour to sample literally.

**Pictograms**

- Isotype rule: more quantity means more same-size symbols, never a bigger symbol, because size changes make comparison unclear. Pictograms are flat and "almost never depicted things in perspective". — [Wikipedia: Isotype](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotype_(picture_language)>)
- Brinton's Fig. 3 (1914) is a pictorial pie chart (cow, house, clothes). His caption warns: "The eye is likely to judge by the size of the pictures rather than by the angles of the sectors." [obs of caption] — [Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Graphic_methods_for_presenting_facts,_p._6-7.jpg)
- Joint Committee rule 2: "represent quantities by linear magnitudes as areas or volumes are more likely to be misinterpreted." — [ASA report](https://archive.org/details/jstor-2965153)

**Fitting long data to the page**

- [obs] On Du Bois's "Number of Negro students taking the various courses of study", the longest (Industrial) bar coils back on itself in a serpentine rather than being truncated or rescaled. — [Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_Georgia_Negro_LCCN2013650436.jpg)
- [inf] This is a striking but one-off device. It works only because the value is also lettered.

## Timeless craft versus period ornament

### Takeaway

The weight hierarchy, direct labels, full titles, few-ink discipline, texture or tint coding of categories, shared-scale small multiples and integrated annotation are all timeless. Most were codified as rules by 1915 and still hold. The engraved cartouches, flourished script, decorative water-lining, double and triple frames, pictorial pies, novelty shapes (spirals, coils) and literal aged-paper colour are period ornament or process artefact. A minimal modern design should not copy them.

### Cited Findings

- Timeless rules written down in 1915: zero line distinguished; "not to show any more coordinate lines than necessary"; curves "sharply distinguished from the ruling"; numerical data in the diagram where useful; lettering readable from the bottom or right; full titles; linear magnitudes over areas or volumes. — [Joint Committee 1915](https://archive.org/details/jstor-2965153)
- Friendly calls the 1880s–90s Albums and Atlases "an exquisite sampler of nearly all known graphical forms". He notes that many of the 1950s–80s "new" methods had "in fact been invented in the 19th century". — [Friendly 2008](https://www.datavis.ca/papers/golden-STS268.pdf)
- Friendly calls the pictogram "one perhaps best forgotten", noting that designers "often got the details wrong, creating a large supply of exemplars of 'bad graphs'". — [Friendly 2008](https://www.datavis.ca/papers/golden-STS268.pdf)
  - Isotype's repetition rule was itself a corrective to scaled pictures. — [Wikipedia: Isotype](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotype_(picture_language)>)
  - Brinton warns that pictures distort pie readings. — [Brinton 1914 p.6](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Graphic_methods_for_presenting_facts,_p._6-7.jpg)
- Brinton saw one-ink hatching as a cost compromise: "The facts in this chart would have been brought out better if colors had been used". — [Brinton 1914](https://archive.org/details/graphicmethodsfo00brinrich)
- Lionni's 1949–51 Fortune redesign aimed to "avoid the kind of false modernity that is sometimes accomplished by graphic tricks". Burtin warned that over-strictness "resulted in rigid, mechanical design". — [Eye](https://eyemagazine.com/feature/article/wheels-of-fortune)
- Minard distorted geography to serve the data (widened Channel, cartoon coastlines). — [Friendly 2008](https://www.datavis.ca/papers/golden-STS268.pdf)

### Inferences

**Keep (timeless)** [inf]:

1. Three-tier line weight:
   - Data marks are heavy.
   - Baseline or reference line is medium or emphasised.
   - Grid is a hairline in a lighter ink, or omitted.
2. Labels on the marks, following the line or set inside the area (Playfair 1786). Endpoint value labels (Du Bois acres chart).
3. A complete descriptive title plus a one-line method or scale note (Playfair footer; Joint Committee rule 17).
4. Categories coded by tint steps of one hue or by texture before reaching for more hues (1874 woodland plate; Brinton). A stepped box legend with explicit numeric ranges.
5. One accent colour reserved for the story series (Minard's black retreat; Cheysson's single blue with red only for notes).
6. Small multiples with identical extent, scale and palette (1883 and 1898 atlases).
7. Annotation integrated into the graphic (Minard's linked temperature strip; Nightingale's explanatory paragraph).
8. Willingness to simplify the base map until it is only scaffolding for data.
9. A warm off-white ground as a choice (Du Bois's buff board, used as an active colour). Pick it as a deliberate neutral, not as simulated ageing.

**Avoid copying (period ornament or process artefact)** [inf]:

1. Cartouches, flourished titles and mixed decorative display faces (Playfair 1801 debt chart).
2. Engraved water-lining and hachure relief that carry no data (1874 atlas).
3. Heavy double or triple frames and graticule bands around non-map charts. A single hairline or no frame suits modern minimal work. Fortune's 4pt black border is a 1930s magazine convention.
4. Simulated foxing, sepia cast and toned paper sampled from scans.
5. Dense hatching or cross-hatch as a stand-in for colour on screen, where tints are free. Brinton himself preferred colour. Keep texture only where it adds a second channel, e.g. for colour-blind or print-mono redundancy.
6. Pictorial pies and scaled pictograms. If using icons, follow Isotype's repeat-unit rule.
7. One-off novelty forms (Du Bois's coiled bar, spirals). They are memorable in an exhibition but break the shared scale.
8. Hand-lettered irregularity as a texture effect. What is timeless is the hierarchy and placement of the lettering, not its wobble.

### Gaps

- None of these sources tests modern readers' responses to "vintage" styling. The keep/avoid split is design judgement grounded in the 1915 rules and the sources' own criticisms, not empirical evidence.
