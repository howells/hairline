# Engrave the hierarchy, not the ornament

An ambitious, old-fashioned Financial Times editor would want a chart system that works like a sub-edited news story. A headline states the finding. A quiet grey line gives the units. One series is set in ink against neutral context. Labels sit on the marks, an exact source closes the chart, and every chart in the paper is built from the same templated frame. The FT's own open-source chart code records this sensibility in exact values. Titles are set in Metric at regular weight, subtitles and sources in warm grey `#66605C`, and gridlines in a darkened paper tone `#e6d9ce`. A short 60×4px black bar is the signature. The print frame uses a 0.3/0.6/2pt rule ladder, and the value axis sits on the right. Hairline already shares most of this DNA: a sans in sentence case, warm paper, one hero ink and 1px hairlines. So the push toward FT sensibility is small and specific, not a restyle. It comes down to five moves. Give the zero or index line full ink against faint grids. Make the title stack carry the point, the units and the source. Use a right-hand value axis and abbreviated years for time series. Add an optional short ink bar or thick–thin rule as a masthead. Keep engraved fills disciplined as tints of one ink, used as a second channel. What makes golden-age and print-era charts look "engraved" is mostly their reproduction process: copperplate, one-ink line blocks and Ben Day tints. Cartouches, double frames, sepia, spaced capitals and the FT's own trademarks (salmon pink plus the black bar plus Metric) are things to leave in the archive. The one real tension is Hairline's ban on in-chart prose, because FT practitioners treat text as critical. The fix is to put the point in the title and allow short labels with values, not sentences, inside the plot.

## An FT editor wants a sub-edited story, not a decorated picture

The FT's chart voice is "restraint without minimalism". John Burn-Murdoch describes a team that is "opposed to 'chartjunk'" and tries "to ensure that our charts contain no more information than they need to". In the same interview he insists that "when you're using charts to communicate, the text is critical". He adds that "if you give it a title that makes a point, you're going to get a stronger reaction", and that print headlines and chart titles "serve the same purpose". The desk brought in **subeditors and headline specialists to write chart titles** ([Eye](https://www.eyemagazine.com/feature/article/conversation-with-john-burn-murdoch)). Alan Smith, head of Visual and Data Journalism, wants charts "in the first few paragraphs", carrying narrative "so that it's not duplicative". He promised "fewer charts, but … better focused" ([Digiday](https://digiday.com/media/financial-times-guide-data-visualization/)). He also argues "there is no such thing as a neutral chart. The decision to make a chart is an editorial decision" ([review of _How Charts Work_](https://www.powerofbi.org/2023/02/05/how-charts-work-understand-and-explain-data-with-confidence-by-alan-smith/)). That is the editor's credo. A chart is an argument with a headline, and authority comes from editorial judgement made visible, not from ornament.

The FT's templates turn this credo into defaults. In `g-chartframe`, every web title is Metric at **`font-weight: 400`** in black, not bold. The subtitle and source are `#66605C`. The placeholder subtitle reads "some supporting information, units perhaps", and a **60×4px black bar** sits top-left as a masthead ([g-chartframe](https://unpkg.com/g-chartframe/build/g-chartframe.js)). The `g-axis` README warns that when values are divided down, "it is very important to make the appropriate addition to the subtitle … e.g. adding the text 'million'". Units belong in the subtitle, not in axis titles ([g-axis](https://unpkg.com/g-axis/README.md)). The two-colour line palette `basicLineWeb` carries the comment "the first colour is neutral / background, the second colour primary/ highlight". It is the code form of the Visual Vocabulary's "Don't be afraid to highlight the points of interest" ([g-chartcolour](https://unpkg.com/g-chartcolour/index.js); [Visual Vocabulary](https://github.com/Financial-Times/chart-doctor/blob/main/visual-vocabulary/README.md)). Rival broadsheets codify the same instincts. The Economist's guide puts "a clear overall message, rather than identifying values for every data point" first and rations bold to "Use rarely" ([Economist style guide 2017](https://sa.ipaa.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/Economist-CHARTstyleguide_20170505.pdf)). The WSJ's Dona Wong asks for thin grids, a slightly heavier zero line, direct labels and at most four lines ([Wong summary](https://www.goodreads.com/en/book/show/6542897-the-wall-street-journal-guide-to-information-graphics)).

"If they could have anything" points toward system rather than flourish. Burn-Murdoch describes coding a chart once and getting "a mobile phone-sized version, a desktop-sized version, a print version" from it, with most charts "heavily templated" ([Eye](https://www.eyemagazine.com/feature/article/conversation-with-john-burn-murdoch)). The Economist locks charts to canonical widths of **160, 332 and 504pt** and exports web versions at 179.22% ([Economist guide](https://sa.ipaa.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/Economist-CHARTstyleguide_20170505.pdf)). That editor would want a small set of fixed frames, fixed type sizes and a fixed rule ladder, so that every chart in the paper is recognisably from one hand. Burn-Murdoch puts the payoff plainly: "anyone can spot an FT chart a mile off" ([Eye](https://www.eyemagazine.com/feature/article/conversation-with-john-burn-murdoch)).

This collides with Hairline's rule of "no in-chart prose", and the collision should be faced honestly. Burn-Murdoch cites research in which minimalist charts ranked lowest with general readers, while a narrative title plus annotations performed best ([GIJN, via search snippet](https://gijn.org/stories/data-visualization-storytelling-tips-john-burn-murdoch/)). Bateman et al. found embellished charts were **recalled significantly better after two to three weeks with no loss of accuracy** ([Bateman 2010](https://vis.csail.mit.edu/classes/6.859/readings/pdfs/Bateman-UsefulJunk.pdf)). The defensible position keeps the ban on sentences inside the plot but moves the argument into the title, which must state the finding in sentence case. Inside the plot, short labels with values and short period tags ("Forecast", "Recession") are allowed. That keeps Hairline's calm plot and still meets the editor's demand that the chart "make a point". Burn-Murdoch himself says the normal-versus-abnormal contrast "can be done perfectly well in static form by simply labelling the lines" ([Flourish](https://flourish.studio/blog/masters-reveal-line-john-burn-murdoch/)).

## Copperplate, lithography and one ink produced the look that now reads as "engraved"

The look a modern designer reads as "vintage" is mostly reproduction process, not styling. Playfair's _Commercial and Political Atlas_ (1786) was printed "by Means of Stained Copper-Plate Charts" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Playfair)). Its Denmark and Norway plate was engraved by Neele of 352 Strand, not by Playfair ([Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:1786_Playfair_-_12_Exports_and_imports_to_and_from_Denmark_%26_Norway_from_1700_to_1780.jpg)). Copperplate "could easily accommodate fine lines, small lettering, stippled textures", but was hand-coloured and so printed in limited numbers. Lithography (1798) made "fine tonal gradation in filled areas" cheaper, and by about 1850 it carried colour ([Friendly 2008](https://www.datavis.ca/papers/golden-STS268.pdf)). Michael Friendly dates the Golden Age to roughly 1850–1900, the era of Minard's 63 graphic works and Cheysson's _Album de Statistique Graphique_ (1879–1897). Cheysson's Album was printed in colour "with great attention to layout and composition" and was dropped after 1897 "due to the high cost of production" ([Friendly 2008](https://www.datavis.ca/papers/golden-STS268.pdf)). The US Statistical Atlases of 1874, 1883 and 1898 made the craft a house style built on "trellis-like collections of shaded maps". Their 1910 and 1920 successors were "routinized productions, largely devoid of color and graphic imagination" ([Friendly 2008](https://www.datavis.ca/papers/golden-STS268.pdf)).

Twentieth-century business print then forced a one-ink discipline that is the true ancestor of Hairline's engraved fills. Willard Brinton's _Graphic Methods for Presenting Facts_ (1914) was printed in one ink because "color printing is prohibitive on account of the cost". He showed "what can be done in printing complex charts with only one color of ink" using cross-hatching, stipple and the "Ben Day method of mechanical shading", and he ordered quartiles as "Light Shading … Dark Shading … and Black" ([Brinton 1914](https://archive.org/details/graphicmethodsfo00brinrich)). Brinton chaired the Joint Committee on Standards for Graphic Presentation. Its 1915 report codified the hierarchy every later broadsheet used. The zero line "should be sharply distinguished". Curves "should be sharply distinguished from the ruling". Show no "more coordinate lines than necessary to guide the eye". Titles should be "as clear and complete as possible". Represent quantities "by linear magnitudes" rather than areas ([Joint Committee 1915](https://archive.org/details/jstor-2965153)). Brinton also showed the rule hierarchy in practice. He criticised a chart whose "100 per cent line has not been made any heavier than the other lines" and praised grids printed in a lighter green ink beneath black curves ([Brinton 1914](https://archive.org/details/graphicmethodsfo00brinrich)).

Newspapers came late to this. A study of comparable US papers found **about 2 charts per edition in 1950–75, rising past 10 by 1980–2005** ([Priceonomics](https://priceonomics.com/when-did-charts-become-popular/)). The FT has printed on salmon newsprint since 2 January 1893, when the paper announced it "will be slightly tinted" to stand apart from the _Financial News_ ([Quartz](https://qz.com/462285/why-the-financial-times-is-pink)). Klim's Metric became the graphics sans in the 2014 redesign, beside the Financier serif for text ([García Media](https://garciamedia.com/blog/financial_times_a_classic_redesign_for_the_digital_age/)). That fact matters for Hairline: **the FT itself sets charts in a sans, not its serif**, so Inter is already on the FT's side of the serif/sans split. Before 2014 the record goes thin. The research found **no primary documentation of FT chart appearance between the 1940s and 1990s**: no scans of Lex charts, no market-page specs, no date for the first colour presses. That FT charts were once black-only on pink, separated by line weight, dashes and Ben Day tints, is plausible general newspaper practice, not a documented FT fact. The documented survival of that era is the current FT print frame. It has black 0.3pt "goalposts" top and bottom, black 0.3pt gridlines, a 0.6pt baseline, 2pt data lines, and all text in black ([g-chartframe](https://unpkg.com/g-chartframe/build/g-chartframe.js); [styles.css](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ft-interactive/visual-vocabulary-templates/master/styles.css)).

The "engraved financial" mood also has a neighbouring source that is not a chart convention. The WSJ's stipple-and-hatch hedcut was adopted in 1979 from Kevin Sprouls. It deliberately emulates "woodcuts from old-style newspapers, and engravings on certificates and currency" ([Wikipedia: Hedcut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hedcut); [Kevin Sprouls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kevin_Sprouls)). It shows that security-print texture signals financial seriousness. But no source ties WSJ, FT, Economist, Bloomberg or Reuters charts to engraved fills. Hairline's hatches are therefore a legitimate extension of Brinton's one-ink tints, not a revival of a known broadsheet chart style.

## Three rule weights, a four-line type stack and a right-hand axis do most of the work

Most of the authority comes from a strict ink ladder rather than any single device. The FT's print ratio is 0.3pt grid to 0.6pt baseline to 2pt data. The Economist puts ticks at 0.4–0.5pt, uses "black for zero baseline only", and makes gridlines the lightest element ([Economist guide](https://sa.ipaa.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/Economist-CHARTstyleguide_20170505.pdf)). The FT's `g-axis` names a thin "axis" style and a thicker "baseline" style for the zero or 100 line ([g-axis](https://github.com/ft-interactive/g-axis)). Economist web charts are print × 1.79, so a 0.5pt tick lands near 0.9 CSS px. **Hairline's pixel-snapped 1px hairline is already the faithful screen equivalent of the print grid**, and the missing step is a baseline in full ink. Running these values against Hairline's current tokens shows where the system already fits and where it slips. (The contrast ratios are my WCAG calculations against `#fbfbf9`, not sourced figures.) The ink `#1d1d1b` gives 16.3:1 and `--muted` `#727068` gives **4.79:1**, which clears the 4.5:1 text threshold of WCAG 1.4.3. But **`--faint` `#9f9e9a` gives only 2.59:1**, and Hairline's `t-note` role uses `--faint` for "keys and units". Under the FT/Economist model, units and sources carry real information. They are the "75k" line, not decoration, so they should move to `--muted`. The grid `#eeece7` (1.14:1) and rule `#e3e1dc` (1.26:1) are close to the FT's own `#e6d9ce` on `#FFF1E0`. W3C allows such faint grids only when values are recoverable from labels or ticks. When readers must use the grid to read values, it falls under the 3:1 non-text rule ([W3C 1.4.11](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/non-text-contrast.html)). The practical ladder has three steps. Faint grids are acceptable as orientation. A baseline that matters needs about 3:1 or more (`--l3` `#7e7d79` gives 3.98:1, ink is better). Any tick label needs 4.5:1. Hairline's 11px axis and note sizes also sit just under Datawrapper's suggested 12px floor ([Datawrapper](https://www.datawrapper.de/blog/fonts-for-data-visualization)). The FT print scale runs down to 7.2pt, but that is a print artefact; on screen, 12px is the safer floor for the source line.

The right-hand axis is the most FT-specific move and the one with a real cost. Cox traces it to Playfair and calls it a commonplace of the _Economist_ and _FT_ ([Stata Journal](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/1536867X1201200314)). The rationale is that the most recent values matter most ([EU Data Vis Guide](https://data.europa.eu/apps/data-visualisation-guide/axes-grids-and-legends)). But right-side labels "compete for the right margin" with end-of-line direct labels ([PolicyViz 2026](https://policyviz.com/2026/08/25/right-side-y-axis-labels-fan-or-foe/)). For Hairline, which leans on direct labels, the rule should depend on the chart type. Use a right axis for single-hero time series, where the hero's end value is the label. Keep the axis left, or put the labels on the gridlines inside the plot, when several series need end labels. The Economist also offers small moves that cost nothing. End the x baseline at the last tick, break axis lines around labels, and drop ticks when segments carry their own values ([Economist guide](https://sa.ipaa.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/Economist-CHARTstyleguide_20170505.pdf)).

Two structural borrowings complete the FT feel. The first is index and double-scale discipline. Index to 100 with a marked line and base dot rather than using twin axes, and where twin axes are unavoidable, "make the zero lines align" ([Economist guide](https://sa.ipaa.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/Economist-CHARTstyleguide_20170505.pdf)). Label log and inverted scales explicitly, because readers shown log-scale COVID charts understood the pandemic _less_ accurately ([Romano et al. 2020](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/hec.4143)). The second is small multiples with identical extent, scale and palette, which is the method of the 1883 and 1898 atlases ([Friendly 2008](https://www.datavis.ca/papers/golden-STS268.pdf)). The Economist sets a fixed 24pt spacer between panels ([guide](https://sa.ipaa.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/Economist-CHARTstyleguide_20170505.pdf)). Burn-Murdoch's "we are not afraid to do a story which just has five line charts in it" is the editorial licence for this ([Eye](https://www.eyemagazine.com/feature/article/conversation-with-john-burn-murdoch)). One last point on colour: Hairline's porcelain palette, with navy ink `#081f5c` on `#f7f2eb` (13.9:1), already sits close to the FT's oxford-blue `#0f5499` hero ([o-colors](https://unpkg.com/@financial-times/o-colors/src/scss/_palette.scss)). It is the natural "FT mode" without borrowing the pink.

## Hatching earns its place as a second channel, never the only one

The historical licence for engraved fills is real but narrow. Playfair separated exports from imports in one ink, with solid black bars against "ribbed" ruled bars ([Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:1786_Playfair_-_Exports_and_Imports_of_Scotland_to_and_from_different_parts_for_one_Year_from_Christmas_1780_to_Christmas_1781.jpg)). The 1874 Woodland plate used **one green ink in five degrees of density** with a stepped I–V box legend carrying numeric ranges ([Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Statistical_atlas_of_the_United_States_based_on_the_results_of_the_ninth_census_1870_with_contributions_from_many_eminent_men_of_science_and_several_departments_of_the_government_LOC_05019329-29.jpg)). Brinton coded each segment of a stacked bar with a different texture and lettered labels inside the blocks ([Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Graphic_methods_for_presenting_facts,_p._6-7.jpg)). Yet Brinton saw hatching as a cost compromise: "The facts in this chart would have been brought out better if colors had been used". He warned that superimposing two cross-hatchings makes a clear drawing "not easy" ([Brinton 1914](https://archive.org/details/graphicmethodsfo00brinrich)). Modern guidance adds that **patterns fail on small marks** such as short bars and thin slices ([Practical Reporting](https://www.practicalreporting.com/blog/2023/9/20/the-tricky-business-of-designing-charts-that-will-be-printed-in-black-and-white)). Greys also imply order, so only about four or five read as distinct. The lesson for Hairline: use texture where it encodes an ordered quantity (density as value, as on the Woodland plate), or where it backs up a label for colour-blind or monochrome readers. Never use texture as the sole carrier of a category.

Hairline's texture code already follows most of the screen-craft rules the research identified. Its patterns use `patternUnits="userSpaceOnUse"`, so pitch stays constant across shapes. Pitches are whole pixels (hatch 4, crosshatch 6, dot screen 4, dense screen 3). Diagonals are drawn as 1px pixel staircases, sidestepping the anti-aliasing that `crispEdges` cannot fix on diagonals ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/SVG/Reference/Attribute/shape-rendering)). Every textured region is outlined with a 1px rule, matching W3C's pie example, where a darker border lets a region pass contrast independently of its fill ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/non-text-contrast.html)). The refinements worth adding are modest. First, keep dot coverage (dot diameter ÷ pitch) under about 0.25. CodeFronts' newsprint default is a 1.15px dot at 6px pitch, and "keep it under about 0.25 or the image fills in". Retune pitch and dot together at 2× ([CodeFronts](https://codefronts.com/design-styles/tailwind-css-grain-texture/tailwind-static-halftone-dot-print-effect/)). Second, when two screens coexist, set them about 30° apart (15°/75° is "the safe pairing"), because near-coincident angles produce moiré "that looks like a rendering bug" ([CodeFronts](https://codefronts.com/design-styles/tailwind-css-grain-texture/tailwind-static-halftone-dot-print-effect/); [Wikipedia: Halftone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halftone)). Third, separate hatched series by both angle _and_ pitch (for example 45° at 4px against 135° at 6px), not by angle alone. Fourth, never use `fill-opacity` on overlapping patterns.

Two cautions follow from the research's inferences. These are design judgement, not sourced rules. The dense screen at 3px sits at the floor where patterns merge into flat grey, so reserve it for the darkest step of an ordered scale rather than a categorical code. Horizontal and vertical rulings at whole-pixel pitch are the only truly crisp hatches on screen. They read as ledger or banknote ruling, which is closer to a financial broadsheet than 45° hatching, so a 0/90° ruling suits shaded periods and highlight bands. Hairline's rule that "the hero is crosshatched or solid ink" fits the FT's two-tone grammar. In the FT-leaning variant the hero should default to **solid ink**, with the crosshatch kept for heroes that are areas. Solid ink stays the one element that never depends on texture.

## What stays in the archive

The golden-age plates mix timeless craft with period ornament. The research separates them cleanly, and the FT adds a third category: its own trademarks. Lionni's 1949–51 Fortune redesign aimed to "avoid the kind of false modernity that is sometimes accomplished by graphic tricks" ([Eye](https://eyemagazine.com/feature/article/wheels-of-fortune)). The same applies to false antiquity.

| Borrow (timeless craft) | Leave (ornament, artefact or trademark) |
| --- | --- |
| Three-weight rule ladder: hairline grid, heavier zero line, heavy data ([Joint Committee 1915](https://archive.org/details/jstor-2965153)) | Cartouches and flourished titles (Playfair 1801 debt chart) |
| Labels lettered on or beside the marks, with no legend (Playfair 1786; Minard 1869) | Engraved water-lining and hachures that carry no data (1874 atlas) |
| A complete title plus a one-line scale or method note | Double and triple frames, graticule bands, Fortune's 4pt black border ([Eye](https://eyemagazine.com/feature/article/wheels-of-fortune)) |
| Tint steps of one ink before adding hues, with a stepped legend giving numeric ranges | Simulated foxing, sepia and colours sampled from aged scans |
| One accent reserved for the story; red held back for notes (Cheysson 1883) | Pictorial pies and scaled pictograms ([Brinton's own warning](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Graphic_methods_for_presenting_facts,_p._6-7.jpg); [Isotype](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotype_(picture_language)>)) |
| Shared-scale small multiples (1883, 1898 atlases) | One-off novelty forms such as Du Bois's coiled bar |
| Base maps simplified to scaffolding (Minard widened the Channel) ([Friendly](https://www.datavis.ca/papers/golden-STS268.pdf)) | Uppercase letterspaced labels (WSJ 0.08em, Bloomberg 0.2em) ([Datawrapper](https://www.datawrapper.de/blog/fonts-for-data-visualization)), which Hairline already bans |
| Warm off-white ground chosen as a neutral | The FT trademark trio of `#fff1e5` salmon, the 60×4 bar _with_ Metric, and "© FT" |
| Tabular lining figures | Oldstyle figures on ticks or tooltips ([Datawrapper](https://www.datawrapper.de/blog/fonts-for-data-visualization)) |
| Abbreviated years, index-to-100, aligned zeros | Literal 6–7pt print type sizes; overlapping cross-hatchings |

Two items need a judgement call. A serif title (Newsreader or Source Serif 4 at an optical display size) is a documented newsroom pattern. The Guardian, Our World in Data and the Washington Post pair a serif title with sans labels ([Datawrapper](https://www.datawrapper.de/blog/fonts-for-data-visualization)). But the FT itself keeps chart titles in Metric, so a serif moves Hairline toward "editorial" in general rather than toward the FT, and it breaks the single-family system. The better FT move is to stay in Inter. Set the title at 500, not 700, and let the subtitle and source carry the broadsheet hierarchy. Display faces such as Fraunces also lack tabular figures ([FontAlternatives](https://fontalternatives.com/fonts/fraunces/)). The FT print "goalpost" frame is authentic and documented: a full-width top rule with 15-unit drops at each end, plus a bottom rule. It suits print exports and dense pages, but on screen cards it duplicates the card edge. Treat it as a print-mode frame, not a default.

## Conclusion

The "old-school FT" quality is not a texture or a typeface. It is an editorial hierarchy expressed in ink, and Hairline can adopt it almost entirely through tokens and roles rather than new marks. The FT itself sets charts in a sans, rules them in hairlines and runs a two-tone neutral-plus-highlight grammar. So the honest route to that sensibility is to tighten what Hairline already does. The baseline goes to full ink. Units and sources rise from `--faint` to `--muted`. Titles become headlines. Time series take a right-hand axis when there is one hero. Porcelain's navy-on-cream serves as the FT-adjacent mode. Engraved fills are the most distinctive part of Hairline and also the least backed by newsroom precedent. Their justification is Brinton's and the 1874 atlas's ordered one-ink tints, which argues for using density to encode order and as backup for labels, never as decoration.

The open risks are evidential. No source documents what FT charts looked like before 2014, how current FT charts compare with the 2017–2021 template values, or whether readers perceive rule weight and restraint as "authority". The keep/avoid line rests on the 1915 rules and on practitioners' judgement, not on reader testing. The cheapest validation is to build one FT-leaning variant of Hairline from the values above and test a headline-titled, single-hero chart against today's version for recall and comprehension. Bateman's results suggest the title and labels, not the ornament, will do the remembering.
